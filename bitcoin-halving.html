Bitcoin Halving: Why Does the Block Reward Keep Falling?

Bitcoin has a monetary system with a predictable issuance schedule. One important part of that schedule is called the halving.

Approximately every four years, the amount of new bitcoin created with each block is cut in half. Let's understand why.

1. What is Bitcoin halving?

Bitcoin halving is an event where the block subsidy paid to miners is reduced by 50%.

The block subsidy is the new bitcoin created according to Bitcoin's protocol and included in a miner's block reward.

Simple example:

Imagine a miner receives 10 new coins for producing a block. After a halving, the subsidy becomes 5 coins. After the next halving, it becomes 2.5 coins.

Bitcoin follows this same idea, but its actual rewards are measured in bitcoin.

2. Why does Bitcoin have halvings?

Bitcoin was designed with a limited supply and a predictable issuance schedule.

Halving gradually reduces the number of new bitcoins entering circulation. This means new supply becomes smaller over time.

The schedule is part of Bitcoin's protocol rules. It does not depend on a central bank or a government deciding how much new bitcoin to create.

3. When does a halving happen?

A halving happens every 210,000 blocks. Blocks are produced approximately every ten minutes on average, so 210,000 blocks take roughly four years.

It is based on the number of blocks, not a fixed calendar date. The exact date of a future halving cannot be known in advance.

4. The history of Bitcoin halvings

Here is how the block subsidy has changed since Bitcoin began.

Year Block subsidy
2009 50 BTC
2012 25 BTC
2016 12.5 BTC
2020 6.25 BTC
2024 3.125 BTC
Next halving 1.5625 BTC

The next halving will happen after another 210,000 blocks have been produced following the 2024 halving.

6. What happens to miners?

Miners use computing power and electricity to compete to produce blocks through Proof of Work.

Miners can receive two kinds of revenue from a block:

  • Block subsidy: Newly issued bitcoin, which decreases at each halving.
  • Transaction fees: Fees attached to transactions included in the block.

When the subsidy falls, miners receive fewer newly created bitcoins per block. Transaction fees remain another source of miner revenue.

Important: A halving does not automatically mean every miner becomes unprofitable. Mining economics also depend on fees, bitcoin's market price, electricity costs and mining equipment.

7. Does halving guarantee a higher Bitcoin price?

No. Halving reduces the rate at which new bitcoin is issued, but it does not guarantee that the market price will increase.

Bitcoin's market price is influenced by many factors, including demand, market conditions, regulation, investor behaviour and wider economic events.

Understanding the issuance schedule is different from predicting the future price.

8. How does this connect to the 21 million limit?

Bitcoin's issuance schedule gradually reduces the block subsidy. Over time, the amount of new bitcoin created becomes smaller and smaller.

The protocol's schedule approaches a maximum supply of 21 million BTC. Because bitcoin is divided into satoshis and subsidy amounts are rounded down to whole satoshis, the exact amount issued is slightly below 21 million BTC.

Eventually, the block subsidy will become zero. Miners can still earn transaction fees for including transactions in blocks.
